                                             Abstract

    The disruption during Covid-19 necessitated statistical organisations across
    the world to impute the missing official statistics data or explore alternative
    data sources. The Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI’s) Inflation Expectations
    Survey of Households (IESH) continued to be conducted (using telephonic
    interviews) during the pandemic months. While imputed figures for the
    official price indices are available at the aggregate level for April and May
    2020, the State-wise inflation data remains unpublished (missing) for four
    of the pandemic months. This paper uses granular IESH data and takes a
    distributional approach to provide State-wise inflation estimates for these
    data gaps. These State-wise inflation estimates when aggregated, closely
    follow the all-India official CPI inflation series.
JEL Classification: D84, E17, E31

Keywords: Consumer price index, distribution function, inflation, inflation expectations

Introduction

        The outbreak of the coronavirus (Covid-19) pandemic across the globe and the
related administrative measures implemented to control its spread, e.g., closure of
non-essential businesses, public offices and restricted movements resulted in a severe
disturbance in the routine compilation and dissemination of official statistics. In such a
scenario, the compilers of official statistics, like Central Banks and National Statistical
Offices across the world needed to retain the confidence of the users of official
statistics in the data. The need of the hour was to ensure complete transparency in the
data collection and dissemination processes. A possible way forward was to make
concerted efforts to collect the required data, drawing on both current and alternative
sources, to compile reliable statistics and cover data gaps using universally accepted
methodologies. Statisticians in various data compiling positions, were proactive in
handling these challenges and exploring new ways to address the gaps in collection
of data. The challenges in the compilation of the Consumer Price Indices (CPI) were
profound. During the Covid period of restricted movement, limited shops were
operational, and several consumer items were unavailable in shops because of supply
chain issues. In addition, restrictions on physical movement made data collection for
the limited available items largely unfeasible. Further, the ability to conduct surveys
was also impacted severely.

        Statisticians around the world had to adapt to these data compilation challenges
and adopt various methodologies available in the literature for addressing missing
data. To fill-in the missing information, data imputation was followed mostly across the
economies. In spite of these, several challenges were being faced by the data users
including Central Banks across the world in analysing these imputed official statistics
which included uncertainty in the imputed figures; changes in the consumption basket
due to pandemic related supply constraints resulting in biased price deflators; delay in
data dissemination; frequent revisions in data and its composition; changes in data
collection methodology; varying seasonal patterns in data; uncertainty in data biases
(e.g., time variation, impact of the changing economy and policy actions); sample
representativeness due to limited access; outdated profile of survey respondents;
changes in time series trends leading to the risk of increasing bias in estimates;
reduced response rates with less accurate responses due to pandemic-related


                                                                                         2
priorities; and constraints related to auxiliary data sharing because of limited resources
and delays due to the pandemic (Beer and Tissot, 2021).

        To address the need for analysing timely data during the pandemic induced
uncertainties, Central Banks required high frequency data from alternative sources in
place of the traditional sources (Hinge, 2020). High frequency data sources were used
to decipher the dynamics of important macroeconomic conditions. The use of such
high frequency data in this regard were proved to be useful in studies by Hopkins and
Sherman (2020) for Ireland, Bank of Japan (2020), Davies et al. (2020) for Bank of
England and Chapman and Desai (2021) for Canada. However, these high frequency
data have their own limitations as they need to be adjusted for seasonality, weather
conditions, and other relevant factors (Castelletti et al., 2020 and Beer and Tissot,
2021). Further, innovative methods needed to be quickly explored for dealing with the
methodological issues induced by the range of vast policy measures being taken by
administrative authorities in response to the pandemic. Beer and Tissot (2021)
suggested better use of existing big data sources, taking advantage of the progressing
digital innovation for building automated link within granular datasets. In spite of all
these efforts, certain gaps remain in official statistics during the pandemic period,
resulting in a break in the time series data.

        While data on several commodity prices were imputed across economies,
including India, there remain certain data gaps during the pandemic period, due to
insufficient data collection. In case of India, imputed price indices for the official
inflation were released at the aggregate level for the months of April and May 2020.
However, the State-wise official inflation figures are missing for four of the pandemic
months. Taking cues from Shaw and Sinha (2024) which highlights the importance of
the granular dataset on the RBI’s IESH in nowcasting and forecasting State-wise
inflation figures, and adaptive nature of the households’ inflation expectations in India,
the current paper makes a novel attempt of using households’ inflation perceptions
and expectations to present urban inflation estimates for the States for the pandemic
months of April and May, 2020 and 2021.

       Section II of the paper reviews various data imputation techniques from the
existing literature followed by cross country estimates and use of alternative data for
imputation. Section III explains the proposed methodology for estimating the missing
State-wise urban inflation data. The first part of Section IV examines the performance
of the proposed methodology using existing data and then proposes State-wise
estimates for the months of April and May 2020 and 2021. This is followed by the
concluding remarks in Section V.

II. Estimation of Missing Time-Series Data

      Missing data may arise due to a random mechanism or a structural deficiency.
The structural deficiency indicates that a component of data is omitted. Random
missing mechanism can be classified into three categories as follows (Kuhn and
Johnson, 2020):

  i.    Missing Completely at Random (MCAR): The missing data is due to a random
        mechanism and the probability of missingness is same for the existing as well
        as missing data. An example of this could be missing price data due to the
        closure of a shop with significant business profile.

 ii.    Missing at Random (MAR): The probability of missingness is not the same for
        the existing and missing data and it depends on the existing data and not on
        the missing data. Observed data can be used to predict the missing data. For
        example, missing data on quarterly gross domestic product (GDP) of a financial
        year can be approximated with the help of available annual GDP estimate and
        past quarterly GDP estimates.

 iii.   Not Missing at Random (NMAR): The missing data is related to the non-
        observed data. The missingness is due to the factors which are not considered
        during data collection. For example, data on sensitive economic variables like
        individual income, savings, etc.

       The missing CPI data during Covid period can be classified as Missing
Completely at Random (MCAR). If the missing mechanism is MCAR and the loss is
low, then ignoring/ deleting the missing data can be a feasible option (Ribeiro and
Castro, 2022). However, the loss in this case is significant and cannot be afforded, as
deletion will result in a break in time series, thereby impacting the entire forecasting
process and analysis. Hence, data imputation becomes necessary for estimating the
missing CPI data for the pandemic period.

       A few imputation techniques following Pratama et al. (2016), Ribeiro and Castro
(2022) and Rocha et al. (2024), are mean, median and mode imputation which replace
the missing data with mean, median or mode of the remaining features (other related
variables) of the data. This is suitable for stationary time series data; hot deck
imputation which searches for data on features like those related to the sample on
missing data and replace the missing data with a random data from the similar sets
(Andridge and Little, 2010); cold deck imputation which replaces the missing data with
a random data from the similar sets from a different data source (Duma et al., 2013);

                                                                                      4
forward and backward filling which replaces missing data with the last observed data
(forward filling) or with the next observed data (backward filling) under the assumption
that missing data is similar to the adjacent data points (Molnar et al., 2008). However,
this method creates an issue if there is seasonality in the variable.

        Other techniques include multiple imputation which calculates multiple imputed
values with a probability distribution for a missing data. These data are examined and
combined to generate realistic estimate (Azur et al., 2011). The method is suitable for
imputing missing data in survey with MAR mechanism. Linear interpolation techniques
have been used to compute using a linear function to connect the nearest points to
represent the data well while spline interpolation has been used for computing missing
values using a polynomial function to connect the nearest points to represent the data
well (Elissavet, 2017). Holt’s (1957) exponential weighted moving average can also be
used wherein, the imputed figure is derived as a weighted average of the historical
data points. The weighting factors reduce exponentially for the older time points. Fix
and Hodges’ (1951) k-nearest neighbour method is appropriate when the distribution
of the data is unknown. The method uses the average or median of the target values
from the k nearest neighbours to make a prediction of the missing value. Kalman
prediction (pioneered by Swerling, 1958, Kalman, 1960 and Kalman and Bucy, 1961)
is a linear model of estimation based on an algorithm and consists of two processes:
a prediction stage and a correction stage. In missing indicators method, for each of
the partially observed feature, a corresponding indicator variable is added to indicate
the missingness using binary values. It can be used with any imputation method, but
is most commonly used with mean imputation (Groenwold et al., 2012). Expectation
maximisation method uses other features to impute the missing value and then checks
whether it is the most probable value. This step is iterated till the most probable value
is found (Dempster et al., 1977). Other recent imputation methods include ST-2SMR
(Cheng and Lu, 2017), temporal belief memory (Kim and Chi, 2018), long short-term
memory (Patterson and Gibson, 2017) and generative adversarial imputation network
(Yoon et al., 2018).

II.1. Cross Country Experiences

       Cross-country experiences show a wide-range of techniques that were used by
various economies to fill-up the gap in commodity prices during Covid-19 period. The
Office for National Statistics in the U.K. imputed 2 the prices of items which were either

unavailable or available in a limited manner. The imputation was based on the method
that best reflected the price behaviour of the missing commodity. The imputations were
based on three methods: first, using the price index immediately above the item in
classification structure; second, using the price movement of a similar commodity; and
third, applying forward filling. For imputing a missing price of non-seasonal
commodities, combined monthly growth of all available non-imputed commodity prices
was used.

       The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics imputed missing prices using cell-relative
imputation method 3. In this method, imputed price is derived using the average price
change of all items similar to the missing item in one month for the same geographic
area. This method is used for commodities where it may be safely assumed that the
price changes in the missing items would be similar to the price changes in the other
items. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics listed down items for which data was
inadequate for publication during each of the pandemic months.

       The Australian Bureau of Statistics used three types of imputation 4 techniques.
For available goods and services, for which prices were temporarily unavailable,
imputed price was taken as the average price change of similar goods and services.
For unavailable goods and services, where a significant amount of its typical
expenditure has been substituted to another good or service, donor imputation
technique was used, wherein price change of a donor commodity is considered. For
unavailable goods and services, where expenditure cannot be substituted by any other
commodity, prices were imputed off the headline CPI.

       The Department: Statistics South Africa (Stats SA) practiced two types of
imputation techniques 5. The price change of commodities for which no expenditure
was possible due to the restricted movement, imputed prices were taken as the change
in the headline CPI index of all items based on those products, for which prices were
either available or imputed by other methods. For the items which are either quarterly
or annually collected, the previous month’s corresponding indices were carried
forward. The price change in diesel was imputed using the change in the regulated
wholesale price of diesel. Certain items under the personal care group were possibly
available for sale, but their prices could not be collected, and therefore, were imputed
by the personal care index.

II.2. Use of Alternative Data Sources for Imputation

       Apart from the above-mentioned methodologies on imputation, there have been
attempts of estimating the missing data by making use of alternative data sources.
Cavallo (2020) used the credit and debit card transactions between the first five
months of 2020 and presented inflation estimates for the U.S. economy. These were
found to be higher than the official inflation. The gap ranges from 0.1 percentage points
in March 2020 to 0.7 percentage points in April 2020 followed by 0.8 in May 2020.
Based on bank card transactions, Gautier et al. (2023) calculated similar estimates for
France from January to December 2020. Their estimates were also on the higher side
with the largest gap being 1.1 percentage points in April 2020. Using debit card data,
Jonckheere and Zimmer (2020) presented such revised estimates for Belgium from
February to July 2020. The estimates were on the higher side than the official inflation
from March 2020 to July 2020.

      Kouvavas et al. (2020) used aggregate turnover data to estimate the changing
consumption patterns and derived revised inflation figures for the Euro area from
January to August 2020. With a finding similar to the above-mentioned ones, the gap
between the estimates and the official inflation peaked to 0.25 percentage points in

III. Methodology

        The use of alternative data sources in computing estimates of the unobservable
economic variables during the pandemic period has been discussed in Section II.
Alternative data may provide only limited information during normal periods of the
economic cycle. However, such data are more indicative of the situation during crisis
periods, more so for the short term (INSEE, 2020). RBI’s IESH data related to
inflationary sentiments may be considered as an alternative dataset and hence, it has
been used extensively in this paper9. The RBI’s Survey of Professional Forecasters
(SPF) is also a rich dataset on all-India inflation forecasts; it cannot be used to study
State-wise inflation estimates.

        This paper attempts to fill-in the missing data on State-wise inflation for April
and May, 2020 and 2021. State-wise inflation data are particularly important for
studying State-specific information like inter-State transportation costs, State taxes,
differences in States for supply chain management, etc., all of which contribute to the
inflation volatility amongst States. The centre-wise survey data are also important for
the study as various factors like respondents’ age, gender, occupation, location, etc.,
impact the sentiments of survey respondents rendering variability in the sentiments
among various centres. Accordingly, the availability of estimates for the State-wise
inflation for the specific months, which remains unpublished, would be potentially
useful for any relevant study involving State-level inflation time-series data going

        Before moving on to fill-in the State-wise inflation data gap, it is necessary to

ahead inflation expectations of consumers are not considered with the logic that such
expectations would likely reflect outdated information in a rapidly evolving economic
environment. The objective is to first examine the performance of inflation nowcasts
using consumers’ inflation perceptions. Thereafter, the performance of three-months
inflation forecasts, which were computed using consumers’ three-months ahead
inflation expectations are to be testified. Given a survey centre for each of the 47
survey rounds above, corresponding State’s official CPI-U data for the previous eight
months are taken as the reference data. For example, if it is required to nowcast CPI-

2024 are considered. Further, for three months ahead forecast, in order to forecast the

        Summing up, the objective was to fill-up the data gaps in the State-wise CPI-U
inflation series during the months of April and May 2020 and 2021, owing to the Covid-
19 pandemic related restrictions. Using centre-wise urban consumers’ inflation
perceptions, three months ahead inflation expectations and past state-wise realised
CPI-U inflation figures, State-wise inflation nowcasts and forecasts were calculated,
and their performances were studied for a fairly long period. It was found that
performance of the proposed State-wise inflation estimates with respect to the
published State-wise MOSPI data of CPI-U inflation figures were comparable.
Accordingly, the State-wise inflation nowcasts and forecasts were obtained for the
months of April and May 2020 and 2021. These estimates were refined using the past
error history adjusting for any error in the State-wise estimates due to sudden onset of
the pandemic and its grossly unknown cascading impact on inflation. As the forecast
error is low compared with alternative estimation forecasts like the interpolation
method, the refined estimates provide a better measure to fill the data gaps during the
Covid period.

V. Conclusion

         The collection and compilation of official statistics suffered acutely during the
outbreak of the Covid-19 pandemic due to the resulting restrictions across the world.
In an environment of uncertainty, scarcity of data made it difficult to clearly understand
the prevailing economic scenario. To address this issue and to retain the confidence
of statisticians and economists in official statistics, statistical organisations attempted
to impute the missing data. In India, State-wise CPI indices could not be collected on
a full-fledged basis during the pandemic months of April and May 2020. As a result,
State-wise CPI inflation figures are unavailable for the months of April and May 2020
and April and May 2021. In this context, this paper uses IESH granular data to derive
State-wise CPI-U inflation estimates for these four pandemic months. These State-
wise inflation estimates are of critical use in the current inflation targeting regime.
Further, these also attempt to resolve the gap in the State-wise inflation series to an

                                                                                           26
extent. When the State-wise inflation estimates are aggregated, the figures closely
follow the all-India CPI inflation series published by MOSPI. Accordingly, State-wise
inflation estimates for the specific months derived in this paper help in bridging the
data gaps. These estimates are expected to be potentially useful for any relevant
study. In future, these State-wise inflation estimates may be compared with model-
based forecasts. The use of non-parametric methods may also be potentially explored
to examine the scope of improvement in the proposed estimates.
