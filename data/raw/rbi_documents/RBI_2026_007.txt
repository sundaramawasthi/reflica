 Global growth remained resilient in 2026 so far, supported by AI-related investment, and fiscal 
stimulus measures, despite disruptions due to the ongoing West Asia conflict. Renewed energy-price 
pressures stoked inflation pressures and prompted a more hawkish stance from leading central bank. 
Fiscal sustainability concerns, persistent inflation, and geopolitical uncertainty raised bond yields, while 
equity markets remained volatile amid lingering concern on valuations technology stocks.
 Amidst global uncertainty, domestic financial markets underwent intermittent volatility during 
H1:2026-27. System liquidity surplus surged in September 2026, mainly on account of the Reserve Bank’s 
swap facilities. Overnight rates in the money market evolved in sync with prevailing liquidity conditions. 
Transmission to lending and deposit rates plateaued with status quo in policy rate during H1:2026-27. 
Bank credit growth became broad based and strengthened further.
 The domestic economy showed resilience in H1:2026-27, despite continued global uncertainty amidst 
the conflict. The supply-side pressures were alleviated by proactive policy measures by the government 
that ensured adequate supply of key inputs. Real GDP recorded a growth of 7.8 per cent in Q1:2026-27 
reflecting strong momentum in underlying economic activity. Aggregate demand remained strong on the 
back of both private consumption and investment. Net external demand was also robust and contributed 
significantly to growth. On the supply side, both manufacturing and services sectors recorded strong 
growth in Q1. High frequency indicators suggest continued buoyancy in domestic economic activity, 
albeit with some moderation compared to Q1. Stable employment conditions, congenial financial 
conditions, revival in corporate investment, and a pickup in goods exports – nurtured by bilateral FTAs 
and diversification – are expected to support economic activity going forward. Sustenance of private 
consumption growth will be essential for broad-based recovery in private capex. 
 Headline CPI inflation increased sequentially, rising above the target during June-August, driven by a 
sustained positive price momentum. Food and fuel groups led to this increase, reflecting adverse weather 
conditions and the sharp rise in energy prices following the West Asia conflict. Core inflation registered 
a pick-up with some signs of generalisation in recent months. Input cost pressures have accentuated, but 
the pass-through has remained limited so far. 
 Going forward, India’s macroeconomic outlook remains resilient supported by domestic demand 
and strengthening investment, despite persistent global uncertainties. The outlook remains vulnerable 
to geopolitical developments, volatility in global energy markets and weather-related uncertainties. Weak 
monsoons coupled with strong El Niño, protracted geopolitical tensions, rising international commodity 
prices, additional frictions in global trade and tightening of global financial conditions could adversely 
impact the growth outlook. Persistent supply-side pressures in food and volatile global energy prices pose 
risks to the domestic inflation outlook, particularly through second-round effects amid strong growth 
impulses. The interplay of these factors will shape the evolving growth–inflation dynamics, with the 
balance of risks depending on developments in global growth, crude oil prices, exchange-rate movements, 
and domestic food supplies.

I. External Environment
Global growth remains resilient, mainly supported by AI-related investment and fiscal measures, but faces renewed 
supply-side shocks from the W est Asia conflict, elevated oil prices and trade related frictions. Inflation has accelerated 
again, prompting central banks to shift towards a more cautious and hawkish monetary policy stance. Financial 
markets remain volatile amid higher yields, geopolitical tensions and shifting risk sentiment.
Global growth has remained resilient despite facing 
persistent headwinds from supply shocks. However, 
the near-term growth forecasts have been revised 
downwards by several multilateral agencies, as 
key supply chains remain disrupted and crude oil 
prices remain elevated. Investment in artificial 
intelligence (AI) and related technologies, along  
with increased international trade in AI-related 
hardware, has supported global economic activity. 
Fiscal support across countries has sustained 
economic activity but also heightened concerns about 
rising public debt and reduced fiscal buffers. Along 
with higher inflation and higher-for-longer monetary 
policy expectations, these concerns have contributed 
to a sharp rise in government bond yields across 
major jurisdictions.
Inflation, which had been moderating towards the 
end of 2025, began to accelerate following the supply 
shock triggered by the West Asia conflict. Crude oil 
prices rose to levels not seen in recent years. At the 
same time, other commodity prices also increased, 
feeding into higher goods inflation even as services 
inflation remained elevated in several major 
economies. Consequently, central banks that had 
been moving towards monetary policy normalisation 
last year adopted a more hawkish stance following the 
onset of the conflict. Several advanced and emerging 
market economies (EMEs) have tightened monetary 
policy or maintained a cautious stance, while only a 
handful have reduced policy rates to support growth. 
Of late, the US Fed and the European Central Bank 
(ECB) have been placing greater emphasis on incoming 
data and their monetary policy frameworks in decision 
making, rather than on forward guidance. The Fed’s 
hawkish stance, combined with elevated crude oil 
prices, contributed to risk-off sentiment and portfolio 
outflows from EMEs, exerting depreciation pressure on 
their currencies. Sovereign bond yields have hardened 
globally, particularly in advanced economies (AEs), 
on concerns over rising public debt, debt-servicing 
costs and persistent inflation concerns. Emerging 
market equities experienced selloffs during Q3 of 
2026, with some recovery in August while advanced 
economy equities gained, driven by technology stocks 
and healthy corporate profits. The US dollar gained, 
as higher bond yields, expectations of a hawkish Fed, 
and safe-haven demand amid geopolitical uncertainty 
outweighed intermittent weakness.

The US personal consumption expenditures (PCE) 
price index, the Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation 
metric, saw inflation surge through May, driven by 
a sharp increase in gasoline prices before falling 
subsequently as energy prices stabilised. In the UK, 
headline inflation has been lower in Q2 2026, mainly 
due to easing food inflation, before edging up in Q3 
so far on rising energy and housing prices. In the 
euro area, energy prices and persistently high service 
inflation drove the CPI inflation higher, except for 
a modest correction in June due to a reversal in 
crude prices. In Japan, inflation rose steadily from 
April, reflecting a slower decline in energy prices as 
subsidies were scaled back in Q2, alongside imported 
inflation from yen depreciation. Inflation rose further 
in Q3, on elevated food prices and transportation 
costs, despite lower electricity prices.
In EMEs, inflation has been divergent, reflecting

with evolving liquidity conditions. G-sec yields 
eased until August before hardening in September, 
reflecting higher global yields, rising oil prices, and 
expectations of monetary tightening. Corporate 
bond yields moved in sync with G-sec yields, with 
a hardening bias, while risk premia showed mixed 
trends. Equity markets gained marginally despite 
heightened volatility in global financial markets and 
crude oil prices due to West Asia conflict. The foreign 
exchange market remained volatile amid shifting US 
policies and lingering conflict in West Asia.  In the 
credit market, both bank and non-bank credit grew 
at a robust pace.
II.2.1 Money Market
During H1:2026-27, money market rates softened 
and remained mostly range bound amid large 
surplus liquidity conditioned by robust capital 
inflows, especially from FCNR(B) deposits under 
the Reserve Bank’s swap facility announced in June 
2026. The weighted average call rate (WACR) – the 
operating target of monetary policy – generally 
remained within the policy rate corridor. However, 
with the significant rise in surplus liquidity, it 
moved downward towards the SDF rate, floor of the 
corridor, in September (Chart II.2a).

Box II.1: Drivers of CD and CP Rate Spreads
Rates on short-term instruments like Certificates of 
Deposit (CDs) and Commercial Papers (CPs) generally 
move in tandem with the policy repo rate but diverge 
at times reflecting broader liquidity conditions and 
funding pressures. Starting from Q3 of 2025-26, 
CD/CP rates hardened and remained elevated even 
as the monetary policy easing cycle continued. It 
resulted in widening of spread of CD/CP rates over 
the policy repo rate. Besides liquidity conditions and 
funding pressures, several other factors can cause 
CP and CD spreads to deviate from their long-run 
averages, including, policy uncertainty, monetary 
policy expectations and market-specific factors such 
as issuance patterns, calendar effects, and episodes 
of market stress (Priyadarshini et al., 2024, Anshul et 
al., 2025). To disentangle the respective contribution 
of various factors which are intertwined and may 
operate simultaneously, uncorrelated shocks are 
identified using historical decompositions within a 
VAR framework using monthly data. The following 
VAR specification is used.
Yt = c + ΦYt–1 + ΓXt + Pεt
 Where Yt includes the selected endogenous variables, 
Xt  contains the exogenous dummies and Pε t are the 
orthogonalised shocks.
The variables are selected to capture the main channels, 
viz., banking system liquidity, funding conditions and 
policy uncertainty through which they can affect short-
term funding costs, while avoiding overlap across 
closely related measures. 7 For CDs, the funding block 
also captures banks’ balance-sheet pressures through 
changes in outstanding credit-deposit ratio and net CD 
issuance growth. For CPs, only net issuance growth is 
used to capture funding conditions. The uncorrelated 
shocks are identified using a recursive identification 
scheme where the most exogenous factors are placed 
at the top while the response variable, i.e., CD or CP 
spreads are placed at the end of the ordering. Historical 
decomposition for the period January 2025 – July 2026 
show the extent of contribution by each identified 
factor to  the movements in spreads (Chart II.1.1).

The decomposition results suggest that the drivers 
of CD and CP spreads have shifted over time. 
Relatively tight liquidity conditions were the main 
driver of widening spreads during early 2025, but 
increased system liquidity along with policy rate 
easing led spreads to moderate to relatively lower 
levels by mid-2025. Since late 2025, the spreads 
hardened again. Funding pressures, owing to stronger 
credit growth relative to deposit mobilisation, 
contributed to hardening of CD spread, while 
economic policy uncertainty was the main driver of 
widening CP spread. More recently, idiosyncratic  
market-specific pressures have also contributed to the 
widening which are unrelated to the identified shocks 
in the model.

Box II.2: Bank Lending Channel of Monetary Policy: A Sectoral Approach
Indian financial system continues to be bank-
dominated. Bank credit as a proportion to GDP 
surged from 52.8 per cent in 2011-12 to 59.8 per cent 
in 2025-26. Therefore, given the importance of the 
banking sector, it becomes pertinent to understand 
the effectiveness of the transmission of monetary 
policy through the lending channel to various sectors 
of the economy. Under the current policy easing cycle, 
bank credit has inched up considerably across sectors. 
Demand conditions for credit have also been strong on 
the back of persisting buoyancy in domestic economic 
activity. In this milieu, it would be important to 
empirically decipher the response of sectoral bank 
credit to monetary policy shocks.
Against this backdrop, the dynamic response of 
overall bank credit and credit to major sectors over 24 
months following an unexpected policy rate change 
is examined using panel local projection technique 
(Jordà, 2005). Policy surprises are extracted using 
data on Overnight Index Swap (OIS) rates around 
RBI policy announcement dates, after controlling for 
current macroeconomic conditions (Lakdawala and 
Sengupta, 2025). Using monthly bank-level panel data 
of 40 banks for the period March 2014 - March 2026, 
the baseline specification for each horizon h = 0, 1, ...,

III. Demand and Output
Domestic economic activity exhibited resilience in H1:2026-27, despite continued global uncertainty. Real GDP 
posted a strong growth of 7.8 per cent in Q1:2026-27. Private final consumption remained healthy, supported 
by buoyant discretionary consumption. Revival in private capex and government’s continued focus on infrastructure 
spending strengthened investment activity. Manufacturing expanded at a robust pace, while services sector 
maintained its buoyancy. Looking ahead, weak monsoons, the prolonged W est-Asia conflict, additional frictions 
in global trade and tighter global financial conditions may have an adverse bearing on both the demand and 
supply side of the economy.
Domestic economic activity exhibited resilience in 
H1:2026-27, despite continued global uncertainty 
amidst the prolonged West-Asia conflict. The supply-
side pressures were effectively dealt with proactive 
policy measures that ensured supply of key inputs 
for productive sectors. As per the National Statistics 
Office (NSO)’s estimates, real GDP recorded a 
growth of 7.8 per cent in Q1:2026-27 reflecting 
strong momentum in underlying economic activity. 
Aggregate demand remained strong on the back of 
both private consumption and investment. Net 
external demand also contributed positively to 
growth with rebound in merchandise exports and 
sustained buoyancy in services exports. On the 
supply side, both manufacturing and services sectors 
recorded strong growth in Q1. Going ahead, weak 
monsoon and El Niño  may weigh on agricultural 
activity. Industry and services sectors are expected 
to continue benefitting from the Emergency Credit 
Line Guarantee Scheme 5.0 and the Transition  
Facilitation (Quality Control) Order 2026. Similarly, 
India’s growing network of bilateral free trade 
agreements (FTAs) augurs well for external demand, 
though uncertainties about additional tariffs on 
US imports, including those under Section 301, 
pose some risk. The risks from protracted West 
Asia conflict and financial market repricing due to  
changes in US monetary policy also require close 
monitoring.

Aggregate demand conditions in the economy 
held up well in Q1:2026-27. As per the statistics

Box III.1: Indian Agricultural Sector amidst Weather Shocks
Rainfall shortfalls remain a major driver of year-to-year 
fluctuation in agricultural output. However, the rural 
economy is increasingly cushioned from erratic  monsoon 
by resilient allied sector activities. Chand and Singh (2026) 
observe that for agricultural households possessing land 
up to one acre, wages contribute more than 55 per cent of 
household income, while crop production and livestock 
together contribute 36 per cent. This suggests that 
rural household incomes are increasingly supported by 
activities beyond crop production. 
Although annual estimates of value added are not 
available separately for rural and urban areas, historical 
benchmark estimates support the use of a broad rural-
linked proxy. In 1999-2000, agriculture, forestry and 
fishing, mining and quarrying, and construction together 
accounted for about 59 per cent of total rural net 
domestic product (Central Statistical Organisation, 2007). 
This exercise therefore uses the sum of GVA of these 
sectors as an indicative proxy for rural-linked activity. 
Data available for both base years ( i.e., 2011-12 and 
2022-23) suggest that the non-agricultural component 
has become more important over time (Chart III.1.1). 
The growth-rate analysis below is calculated within the 
relevant published GVA base, with 2023-24 onward using 
the 2022-23-base series. 
Rainfall sensitivity differs across the two components of 
the proxy, viz., agricultural and non-agricultural (Chart 
III.1.2a). Agricultural growth suffers more in years of higher 
monsoon deficit, while growth in the non-agricultural 
component stays close to 6 per cent at every cut-off. In 
years of surplus rainfall, by contrast, agricultural growth 
averages 5.1 per cent, above the 4.3 per cent recorded in 
years close to the LPA. It is estimated that non-agricultural 
activity was the predominant contributor to growth in 
rural economic activity during years of rainfall deficiency 
exceeding 7 per cent, as against in other years.
The same contrast is evident in the regression estimates 
(Chart III.1.2b). Over 1994-95 to 2025-26, a regression

at 1 per cent. The shortfall measure explains about 39 
per cent of the variation in agricultural growth. For the 
non-agricultural component, the coefficient is 0.01 but 
not statistically significant (Chart III.1.2b). The pattern 
is also stable across two equal sub-periods. Including

IV . Prices and Costs
Headline CPI inflation increased sequentially, rising above the target during June-August driven by a sustained 
positive price momentum. Higher food and fuel inflation led to this increase, reflecting adverse weather conditions 
and the sharp rise in energy prices following the W est Asia conflict. Core inflation registered a pick-up with signs 
of generalisation in recent months. Going ahead, persistent supply-side pressures in food and volatile global energy 
prices pose risks to the domestic inflation outlook, particularly through second-round effects amid strong growth 
impulses.

The headline consumer price index (CPI) inflation 1 
has been increasing steadily in the period since the 
release of the previous MPR in April 2026. From 3.4 
per cent in March 2026, headline inflation increased 
over successive months to 4.4 per cent in June (the 
first print above the inflation target in seventeen 
months) and further to 4.8 per cent in August 
(Chart IV.1a).

Box IV.1: Measuring Inflation Generalisation through the Diffusion Index
When confronted with supply shocks, monetary 
policy responses are calibrated based on the 
assessment of second-round effects. If supply 
shocks are temporary, monetary policy often looks  
through them, whereas when there are signs of 
generalisation, policy acts to prevent inflation from 
becoming embedded in expectations. One critical 
challenge in this context is to gauge the extent to 
which inflation has become generalised. This can 
be assessed by examining whether the initial price 
pressures remain confined to the directly affected 
components or have spread to a wider set of items in 
the CPI basket. 
A useful measure of this generalisation is the 
inflation diffusion index , which captures the 
weighted distribution of item-level price changes in 
the CPI basket (de Soyres et al., 2026). An increase in 
the diffusion index in the high inflation categories 
indicates that price pressures are becoming more 
widespread across the consumption basket. This 
can be used along with the headline or core 
inflation numbers to understand the nature of 
inflation process. An increase in headline inflation 
accompanied by a low diffusion index for the high 
inflation categories is indicative of inflation driven by 
idiosyncratic supply shocks. On the other hand, surge 
in diffusion index in the higher inflation category 
along with the headline inflation number, indicates 
that inflationary pressures have become more broad-
based and generalised. 
Many countries use diffusion indices alongside other 
measures of underlying inflation to understand the 
spread of inflationary pressures. 25 Over the years, 
the Reserve Bank of India has also examined the 
distribution of item-level inflation using diffusion 
indices, inflation distributions and kernel density 
plots (RBI, Monetary Policy Report, various issues; 
Nadhanael and Pattanaik, 2010; Hasan et al, 2026). 
Against this backdrop, the box attempts to compile 
diffusion indices for inflation in India based on the 
revised CPI (2024=100) series. The diffusion indices 
are compiled for i) Headline CPI comprising all items; 
and ii) Core or CPI excluding food and fuel. 26 The 
indices are constructed using weighted item-level 
year-on-year (y-o-y) inflation rates. 27
Conceptually, diffusion indices represent the 
weighted share of items in the respective CPI basket, 
categorised into the following four buckets (intervals), 
aligning it with the inflation target and its tolerance 
band:
i.  Below 2 per cent;
ii.  Between 2 per cent and 4 per cent;
iii.  Between 4 per cent and 6 per cent; and
iv.  Above 6 per cent.

Box IV.2: What Drives Inflation in India? Disentangling Demand and Supply Factors
Disentangling the relative roles of demand and 
supply factors in driving inflation is critical for an  
inflation-targeting central bank to calibrate appropriate 
policy response. Demand-driven inflation typically 
calls for monetary tightening, while supply-driven 
inflation creates difficult policy trade-offs as it is often 
accompanied by a slowdown in growth. In case of 
India, the recent experience of inflation significantly 
undershooting the target before a sharp reversal 
merits a thorough examination of the role of demand 
and supply factors in shaping the inflation dynamics.
Empirical literature has adopted different approaches 
to disentangle the contributions of aggregate demand 
and supply conditions such as identifying the cyclical 
component of inflation by examining its relationship 
with economic activity as proposed by Stock and 
Watson (2020). Another approach is to explicitly 
decompose inflation into demand and supply-driven 
components using price and quantity information 
at the commodity or consumption-category level 
(Shapiro, 202433 and Shekhar, Kishore and Bhoi, 2023).
More recently, Eickmeier and Hofmann (2025) have 
developed a structural factor model to identify 
aggregate demand and supply conditions using a 
broad set of inflation and real activity indicators. Their 
methodology exploits the common variation across a 
large number of macroeconomic series and identifies 
demand and supply factors through theoretically 
motivated sign restrictions on factor loadings. 
The demand factor is characterised by positive co-
movement between inflation and economic activity, 
whereas the supply factor is associated with opposite 
movements in inflation and economic activity. This 
framework enables the construction of aggregate 
indicators of demand and supply conditions and 
facilitates an assessment of their contribution to 
inflation dynamics over time.
This factor-based approach is applied to the Indian 
economy to identify indicators of aggregate demand 
and supply conditions and examine their role in 
the evolution of inflation in India. Compared with 
approaches that focus primarily on cyclical inflation 
or commodity-level price and quantity responses, the 
proposed framework incorporates information from a 
broader set of macroeconomic variables. 
Data
The dataset includes a broad set of inflation measures 
including headline CPI, house price index, and other 
group/sub-group-wise inflation. The real-activity block 
includes GDP and expenditure components, gross and 
sectoral GVA indices, and other demand proxies such 
as diesel consumption and capacity utilisation. Other 
global price indicators like gold, copper, Brent crude 
oil and fertiliser prices are also included. 34

by employing principal component analysis on

IV.5 Conclusion
Headline inflation has increased steadily throughout 
2026-27 (up to August), initially driven by higher food 
and fuel inflation but showing signs of broad-based 
price pressures in recent months. The impact of 
El Niño conditions and deficient southwest monsoon 
is increasingly reflected in food prices. Core inflation 
recorded a pick-up along with incipient signs of 
generalisation. Going forward, there is a material 
risk that supply-side pressures in food may remain 
unabated as weather uncertainties persist and global 
oil prices continue to remain volatile. In this context, 
generalisation of inflation through second-round 
effects remains a major risk from the firming up of 
demand conditions, given strong growth impulses. 
This is particularly pertinent as growth in credit and 
monetary aggregates remains robust (see Chapter II); 
therefore, stricter vigilance is required on the inflation 
front.

Chapter V Macroeconomic Outlook
V . Macroeconomic Outlook
Amidst persistent geopolitical tensions, volatility in global commodity and financial markets and persisting trade 
uncertainty, India’s economic activity remains robust, supported by resilient domestic demand, strengthening 
investment and sound macroeconomic fundamentals. Headline inflation has firmed up in recent months, largely 
reflecting food and fuel price pressures, but with increasing evidence of generalisation. The outlook for growth 
and inflation is subject to uncertainties arising from external economic environment, commodity prices, weather 
events and global financial conditions. Against this backdrop, monetary policy remains focussed on maintaining 
price stability while supporting sustained economic growth.
V.1 Key Developments since the April 2026 MPR  
Since the release of the Monetary Policy Report (MPR) 
in April 2026, the global economy has continued to 
navigate an uncertain environment precipitated 
by geopolitical tensions, evolving trade and tariff 
stipulations, volatile financial conditions, and rapid 
technological change. Despite these headwinds, 
global economic activity has remained resilient, with 
the International Monetary Fund (IMF) projecting 
global growth at 3.0 per cent in 2026 and 3.4 per cent in 
2027.1 The inflation outlook, however, is of material 
concern with global headline inflation projected to 
rise from 4.1 per cent in 2025 to 4.7 per cent in 2026 
by the IMF , suggesting that the disinflation process 
that was underway since early 2024 has reversed 
its course. Macroeconomic dynamics have been 
multi-paced across countries, reflecting the varying 
level of exposure to geopolitical developments, 
state of domestic demand conditions and extent 
of involvement in the ongoing technology-related 
investment cycle. 
Against this backdrop, monetary policy has been 
cautious in major economies with central banks 
balancing the risk of renewed inflationary pressures 
against concerns on slowing economic activity. 
Financial markets, meanwhile, have remained 
resilient, albeit with intermittent bouts of volatility 
in the wake of the prevailing uncertainty. Global 
equity markets have generally been supported by 
strong corporate earnings and optimism around 
technology-related investment, even as geopolitical 
uncertainties and macroeconomic data releases 
led to periodic corrections. Sovereign bond yields, 
particularly at longer maturities, have hardened 
across major economies amidst concerns over 
inflation, elevated public debt and large financing 
requirements. 
Global commodity prices have been volatile since 
April 2026, with the West Asia conflict induced initial 
surge moderating across some segments before 
renewed supply concerns triggered price pressures 
again since August. Energy markets were particularly 
sensitive to supply disruptions. Similarly, metal 
prices remained elevated amidst supply disruptions, 
supported by strong demand from infrastructure and 
technology-related investment, before moderating 
subsequently and rising again since August. 
The emergence of El Niño  conditions has also 
added uncertainty to the outlook for agricultural 
production and food prices. Agricultural commodity 
prices, which were relatively stable after April,

On the domestic front, economic activity remained 
robust in Q1:2026–27, with real GDP expanding by 
7.8 per cent and real GVA by 8.2 per cent. Growth 
remains broad-based, with strong momentum 
in industry and services, while agriculture and 
allied activities also contributed positively. On the 
expenditure side, private consumption remained 
resilient and investment activity strengthened, with 
gross fixed capital formation registering a notable 
expansion.
Headline CPI inflation increased from 3.5 per cent 
in April 2026 to 4.8 per cent in August, driven by 
sustained positive price momentum. Food inflation 
firmed up during this period and contributed 
significantly to the increase in headline inflation. 
Fuel inflation, which had remained subdued 
earlier, started increasing from May due to elevated 
international energy prices and their pass-through 
to domestic prices, albeit partially. Core inflation 
recorded only a modest increase till July before 
accelerating in August amidst signs of generalisation.
Against this growth-inflation dynamics, the Monetary 
Policy Committee (MPC) considered it prudent to 
wait for the implications of prevailing uncertainties 
on growth and greater clarity on the inflation 
outlook to emerge before undertaking any policy 
action. Therefore, following the cumulative easing of 
125 basis points during February to December 2025, 
the MPC left the policy repo rate unchanged at 5.25 
per cent in its April, June and August 2026 meetings 
while retaining the neutral stance.

The MPC met for its first policy of the financial year in

domestic economy, the MPC considered it prudent 
to wait and unanimously decided to keep the repo

neutral stance.

Factors conditioning the Macroeconomic Outlook
Macroeconomic developments pertaining to 
economic activity and inflation during H1:2026-27 
are analysed in chapters III and IV. Going forward, 
the outlook is based on a set of baseline assumptions. 
First, assumption on crude oil price has been revised 
up to US$95 per barrel for the second half of 2026-
27 from US$85 per barrel for 2026-27 in the last 
MPR (Table V.2). This makes the alternate scenario 
presented in MPR April 2026 as our baseline with a 
$10 per barrel rise in oil price assumption over 2026-
27 and 2027-28. International crude oil prices peaked 
in April 2026 primarily because of severe supply 
shock caused by the West Asia conflict. Following the 
signing of Memorandum of Understanding between 
the US and Iran in June, oil prices eased and reached 
around US$83 per barrel in July 2026. After the 
ceasefire violations and the consequent collapse 
of the US-Iran agreement, prices began to firm 
up in August and further escalated in September. 
The Brent futures curve, however, remained in 
backwardation, with expectations of prices easing in 
future (Chart V.1a). The upward pressure on prices 
was mitigated to some extent due to destocking of 
crude oil inventory by several countries, which has 
witnessed sharp depletion since Q4:2025-26, with 
minimal support to prices expected in the months 
ahead. With steady increase in production, crude 
oil inventory is expected to be replenished in the 
second half of 2026-27 that might keep demand and 
price pressures high (Chart V.1b).
Second, the nominal exchange rate of the Indian 
rupee (₹ ) remained in the range of ₹ 92–97 per US 
dollar during April–September 2026 (Chart V.1c). The 
rupee depreciated towards the end of April driven by 
portfolio outflows that reflected a risk off sentiment 
towards EMEs under heightened uncertainty. 
Subsequently, the rupee reversed its trend, 
supported by the easing of geopolitical tensions and 
the announcement of measures by the Reserve Bank 
of India to attract foreign capital flows including 
USD-INR swap window for FCNR(B) deposits and 
foreign investment in government securities. The 
Indian rupee subsequently weakened in early July 
in tandem with rising crude oil prices, but has since 
remained range-bound. Considering the volatility of 
the US dollar and fluctuations in global capital flows, 
the baseline assumption for the exchange rate has 
been revised to ₹ 95 per US dollar as against ₹ 94 in 
the April 2026 MPR.

V.2 Inflation Outlook 
Domestic headline inflation edged up to 4.8 per cent 
in August, driven primarily by rising food and fuel 
prices but with increasing evidence of broad-basing 
of price pressures as evident from diffusion indices 
(as discussed in Chapter IV). Inflation in food and 
beverages rose to 5.7 per cent, while core inflation 
inched up to 4.2 per cent. Going forward, inflation 
trajectory remains subject to considerable upside 
risks from overlapping climate and geopolitical 
shocks. Domestically, outlook is clouded by 
recent projections from the National Oceanic and 
Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), USA, warning 
that the prevailing El Niño  phenomenon is set to 
intensify till the end of 2026 and remain strong 
in the first half of 2027, posing downside risks to 
agriculture output, and reservoir replenishment.

impact on global energy prices, commodity markets 
and supply chains. Renewed geopolitical tensions 
have kept energy markets volatile and could raise 
freight, insurance and other transportation costs.

domestic retail prices and raise input costs across a 
range of products, triggering the risk of second-round 
and broader cost-push pressures.

V.4 Balance of Risks
The baseline projections of domestic growth and 
inflation remain conditional on key domestic and 
global macroeconomic assumptions that are given 
in Table V.2. These assumptions are subject to 
evolving uncertainties from ongoing geopolitical 
tensions in West Asia, commodity price volatility, 
global trade fragmentation, and domestic weather-
related risks. Against this backdrop, this section 
outlines the balance of risks surrounding the 
baseline projections under plausible alternative  
scenarios. 
(i) Global Growth Uncertainties
Global economic activity is navigating crosscurrents 
from ongoing war-induced supply shocks and a 
positive technology-led investment cycle. Growth 
momentum remains highly uneven, with advanced 
and emerging economies that are integrated into 
the artificial intelligence (AI) and semiconductor 
value chains experiencing technology-driven growth. 
In contrast, activity across energy-importing and  
non-tech economies are constrained by elevated 
energy prices, structural headwinds, and trade 
disruptions.
Downside risks to global growth dominate. 
Broadening geopolitical conflicts in the West Asia 
may continue to disrupt global supply chains and 
critical maritime transit routes, particularly the Strait 
of Hormuz. This continued escalation of geopolitical 
hostilities may further disrupt the commodity 
markets. Moreover, elevated policy uncertainty 
and a potential surge in trade protectionism 
may restrain international trade flows and  
investment. 
High public debt levels in major economies 
leave sovereign markets exposed to financial 
tightening, restricting the fiscal space for productive  
investment. Downside risk also originate from 
potential sharp downside corrections in AI-related 
asset valuations which pose contagion risks to global 
financial markets. 
On the upside, a durable resolution of geopolitical 
tensions, swifter normalisation in energy markets, 
and faster AI-driven productivity gains could lift 
global growth above the baseline.

Box V.1: Crude Oil Price Shocks: Quantifying the Macroeconomic Impact on the Indian Economy
Being an energy importer, crude oil price dynamics 
remain a critical determinant of macroeconomic 
stability in India, exerting a profound influence on 
domestic price stability, external sector balances, and 
overall growth outlook. Global crude oil prices have 
experienced extreme volatility in 2026 so far, driven by 
the West Asian conflict and supply chain disruptions. 
The Indian crude oil basket spiked to around US$125 
per barrel in March 2026. Prices subsequently eased to 
an average of US$82 per barrel in July, before rising to 
US$90 per barrel by August 2026. The renewed West 
Asia conflict has pushed up crude oil prices to $116 per 
barrel in September 2026.
Given India’s heavy reliance on external energy 
sources, adverse international crude price shocks 
pose significant pass-through risks to headline 
inflation – both directly through fuel components 
and indirectly via generalised cost-push pressures, 
creating headwinds to medium-term macroeconomic 
stability. Furthermore, the broader global energy 
outlook remains clouded by several compounding 
factors including widespread uncertainty regarding the 
timeline for supply resumption and the restoration of 
critical shipping routes. Additionally, global strategic 
petroleum reserves remain low, leaving markets 
vulnerable to further shocks. 
To evaluate how these price fluctuations ripple through 
the economy, the Quarterly Projection Model (QPM) is 
utilised to delineate several interconnected channels 
through which the increase in crude oil prices impacts 
the economy (John et.al., 2022, 2023). 
An escalation in crude oil prices adversely impacts key 
global macroeconomic aggregates, aggravating global 
inflationary pressures, dampening output growth, 
possibly triggering higher monetary policy rates. 
Heightened global energy prices cause import bill to rise, 
putting downward pressure on the domestic currency, 
leading to rise in imported inflation. An immediate 
pass-through of international price movements to 
domestic energy prices exert an instantaneous upward 
pressure on headline inflation, capturing the direct 
effect. The increase in petroleum product prices 
triggers indirect cost push effects emanating from firms 
passing on higher input costs, pushing up prices of 
final goods and services. Concurrently, elevated energy 
prices compress household real disposable incomes 
and narrow corporate profit margins, precipitating 
a contraction in discretionary consumer spending 
and private investment. This resultant moderation 
in aggregate demand has a dampening effect on 
inflationary pressures.

uptick in mid-2026, driven primarily by food price

and erratic monsoon experienced in 2026-27.

V.5 Conclusion
India’s macroeconomic outlook continues to be 
supported by resilient domestic economic activity 
on the back of private consumption and investment. 
The external environment, however, remains 
uncertain. At the same time, improving investment 
outlook, bilateral trade agreements and conducive 
financial conditions could provide further support to 
economic activity. On the inflation front, the recent 
increase in headline inflation has been driven largely 
by food and fuel prices, but core inflation has also 
registered a pickup from its benign levels, indicating 
signs of greater generalisation of price pressures 
as evident from diffusion indices. Going forward, 
upside risks to the inflation outlook persist from 
renewed geopolitical tensions, disruptions to energy 
supplies and adverse weather conditions. Against this 
backdrop, potential second-round effects warrant 
stricter vigilance particularly as demand conditions 
remain strong. Overall, the outlook for growth and 
inflation will depend on how domestic demand and 
supply conditions evolve alongside developments 
in the global economy, with the balance of risks 
remaining contingent on these factors.
